Brand images

Fifty images in six families named for the job they do, not the objects in them — chosen as one coherent photographic world: candid, human-centered, and free of AI clichés. Strongest first, whatever family they come from. Search by what is in the picture, or filter by family.

The photography style

The library is built as one photographic world in named families — the filter row above the grid is the current list — not a pile of images that happen to look alike. Consistency comes from lighting, composition, authenticity and subject matter, not from forcing every frame through the same color grade: a slightly imperfect photo of two people genuinely working beats an exquisitely lit photo of five models pretending to meet.

The families are named for where an image goes, not for what is in it. An earlier split by subject matter — collaboration, data, workspaces — put the same photograph in three plausible places, because a picture of three people at a dashboard is all of those at once. Asking which page does this go on has one answer per image, so a family is now a shortlist you can hand to whoever is building the page.

RuleWhat it means when choosing an image
The house compositionOne person, mid-shot, holding the tool of their work and looking down at it, with a real place softly out of focus behind. This is what rbaconsulting.com uses almost to the exclusion of everything else, and it is the most useful tiebreaker when two candidates are otherwise equal. It is not the only composition here — the collaboration family is deliberately built on two and three people — but when in doubt, match this.
Candid over posedPeople engaged with the work or each other, never the camera. A photograph is candid when you could remove the photographer and nothing in the scene would change.
Human-centeredThe person always outranks the machinery, the screen and the office. An engineer with a tablet beats a dramatic robot arm with nobody around.
Environmental portraitsShow people in their working context with the tools of their craft — not studio headshots, not arms-folded executives.
Technology in human usePhotograph people using software and devices, and make the screen believable: real code, a real spreadsheet, a real document. No robot heads, glowing brains, blue circuitry faces, holograms or neon cyber-tunnels — they undermine RBA’s pragmatic AI positioning. This rule governs photography; backgrounds are a separate job with separate rules.
Contemporary & realNatural light, modern workplaces, believable clutter. If it feels obviously like stock, it fails, however beautiful it is.
Dressed for the actual roomBusiness casual: shirts and knitwear, no suits and ties, no PPE. Clothing is the fastest signal of which industry and which decade a photograph belongs to, and a boardroom of suits reads as banking rather than consulting.
Range is a rule, not a nicetyJudge the library, not only the frame. Age, gender and ethnicity should vary across a family, and so should the kind of work being done — eight designers at monitors is a narrow library however good each picture is.
No forced gradingUse documentary images as supplied. Trying to make everything beige, blue or desaturated makes a library feel more artificial, not less. This is a rule about this tier, not a ban on treatment: a frame that is deliberately graded belongs in Signature & campaign — see Treating an image.

Rejected on sight

Each of these killed at least one real candidate in the last pass and most killed several, so they are worth knowing by name: a reviewer who can spot the trope works far faster than one applying the rules above from scratch. Several had also crept back into the library after an earlier review removed them, which is the argument for writing them down rather than re-deriving them each time.

The tropeWhy it fails
Eye contact with the lensThe moment someone looks at the camera the picture becomes an advertisement. Attention belongs on the work or on the other person — RBA’s own photography almost never makes eye contact.
The foreground-blur sandwichThe subject framed between two out-of-focus heads, shoulders or pot plants. It is meant to read as fly-on-the-wall and instead announces a shoot, because nobody actually stands there. The most common single fault in candidate sets.
Faces cropped by the frame edgeA head cut off at the top of the frame, or a torso with no face at all. These break the human-centered rule while claiming to illustrate people, and cropping cannot fix them.
Dead or invented screensA black monitor, a blank screen being gestured at, or a dashboard whose chart could not plot real data. If the screen is not plausible the photograph is about nothing — and on a technology consultancy’s site that is the one thing it cannot be.
Corporate theatreHandshakes, high-fives, fists raised at a milestone slide, colleagues striding past a glass building, anyone pointing at a chart. Nothing is happening in these pictures; they are gestures performed for a camera.
Hi-vis and hard hatsUsually real photographs, and still wrong. PPE reads as heavy-industry contractor rather than consulting, and it is a register rbaconsulting.com never uses. Show the plant manager in a shirt on the floor instead.
The cinematic dark gradeTeal-and-orange, dark server aisles, moody control rooms. Individually striking, collectively impossible — they cannot sit beside daylight frames in one layout. This fault has now returned twice under different contributors’ names, so check for it on every intake.
One idea photographed twiceJudge a candidate against the family it is joining, not on its own. Four takes on “person with a tablet in an aisle” is one image and three wasted slots. ./tools/images-sync.py --review catches these by contributor, category and title.
The AI tellsImpossible dashboard graphics, plastic skin, hands that do not resolve, suspiciously symmetrical clutter, nonsense text on signage, a blank screen where content should be. Useful for narrowing a contact sheet quickly — but they are a shortlist aid, not a verdict. Eight generated images of people passed a careful visual review and shipped. The only reliable check is the Generated with AI line on the asset page; see the licensing table below.

Backgrounds are a different job

RuleWhat it means when choosing an image
A surface, not a subjectA background exists to be put something on top of — a headline, a stat, a section title. Judge one on its quiet area, not its busiest corner: if there is nowhere for type to sit, it is a texture, not a background. Never run one as a hero on its own, and never pair two in the same layout.
Depth, not flatnessThe family is built on a dark ground, geometric repetition, and depth you can see — focus falling off, atmosphere, perspective. That is the difference between the abstracts that work here and the plexus-network cliché: both are geometric, but one has a real camera or a real render behind it and the other is a flat vector pretending. Glowing node diagrams, particle waves, neon fibre and data tunnels stay banned. A grid photographed from a helicopter at dusk does not.
Amber against blueThe accent relationship that holds the family together: warm light inside a cool structure. Lit windows in a dark facade, street grids between blue blocks, warm blocks on a cool data surface. It gives a layout somewhere for a highlight to sit without introducing a new colour, and it is why these read as one set rather than six unrelated textures.
Generated is allowed hereBackgrounds may be AI-generated or 3D-rendered, because no human being is depicted — there is no model release to be missing and nobody being misrepresented. This is the one place the library permits it. Record it on the entry (gen) so the next person does not have to work it out, and keep the licensing checks below in full: generated does not mean unencumbered.
Check for baked-in typeStock background graphics often ship with sample words set into the artwork. Every image that did has now been cut, so nothing in the current set carries one — but the check does not retire with them. Look at all four corners before using one, and if you find words, crop them out or delete them in the vector. Shipping someone else’s stock headline is worse than shipping no image.

Treating an image

A treated frame is a photograph carrying a deliberate grade or a graphic overlay. It lives in its own family, Signature & campaign, alongside the documentary families — so the filter row above the grid is how you see one register at a time.

Filing it correctly is what makes treatment safe. Grading a documentary library to force coherence is the thing the rule above bans, and it is still banned — a graded frame filed among documentary work is one that will eventually sit beside an ungraded frame in the same layout and look like a mistake. Keeping the register in its own family is the whole difference between a deliberate choice and a library that has quietly drifted blue.

Two duotones, not five. A treatment set only reads as a system while it is small enough to recognise, and these two already cover the two jobs: one takes reversed type, one takes dark type. Both are anchored on Primary so a treated frame and an untreated heading share a colour.

SpecValue, and how to reproduce it
Deep duotoneShadows #1D366D, highlights #14BADB. On the web: filter: url(#rba-duotone-deep). In Photoshop: Image → Mode → Grayscale, then Image → Mode → Duotone with those two inks. In Figma: a Luminosity-blended fill over a grayscaled image.
Soft duotoneShadows #1D366D, highlights #BDE4F1. Same routes, second stop swapped.
How it is builtLuminance first (Rec.709: 0.2126 R + 0.7152 G + 0.0722 B), then a two-stop ramp per channel. Never a coloured layer at reduced opacity — that washes the whole image toward one hue and leaves the shadows muddy, where a true duotone keeps full contrast.
Overlay strokeAqua #14BADB, 2–3px at 1×, with a 0 0 12px rgba(20, 186, 219, 0.55) glow. One weight, one colour, one glow — an overlay that varies per image is decoration, not a system.
The overlay must mean somethingA drawn line is allowed when it traces something real in the frame: a route, a flow, a connection between two things that are actually there. A glowing swoosh thrown across a photograph because it looked empty is the single fastest way to date this library, and it is the failure mode of every competitor doing this badly.
Never treat a face into abstractionGrade a person, do not dissolve them. The human-centered rule outranks the treatment: if the grade has made it unclear what someone is doing, the treatment has failed regardless of how good it looks.
One treated frame per layoutSame discipline as backgrounds. Two treated images on one page read as a filter applied to a folder rather than a decision made about a picture.
File it rightA treated frame belongs in Signature & campaign, not in the subject family it happens to depict. Filed among documentary work it will be found by someone looking for a straight photograph and used as though it were one.

Provenance & rights

Cost is handled elsewhere and deliberately is not on this page. A library reads differently when every card carries a price, and picking an image should be an editorial decision rather than an accounting one. What follows is the part that holds whatever the budget is.

CheckWhat to confirm
Generated imagery: the ruleAn image may be AI-generated only if no human being is depicted in it. Backgrounds, textures, architecture and other surfaces are fine generated — nobody is being portrayed, so there is nothing to misrepresent. Photography of people is never fine generated: it carries no model release, no real workplace and no accountability, and a brand library that shows invented colleagues is claiming something untrue about the firm.
How to actually checkOpen the asset page and read the line directly under the contributor’s name. Adobe prints Generated with AI there, between the byline and DIMENSIONS, on every declared asset. That is the authoritative signal and it takes one look. Record it: entries in library.json carry a gen field so nobody has to re-derive it later.
Do not screen by pixel dimensionsAn earlier version of this page recommended judging AI by file size, on the theory that generated output is small and lands on multiples of 64. That guidance was wrong and has been removed. Eight declared-AI images passed it and shipped — at 6208 × 3104, 7600 × 4000 and 8256 × 4608, sizes indistinguishable from a camera file. Generators now emit whatever dimensions are asked of them. Dimensions tell you whether an image is big enough to use, and nothing whatever about how it was made.
The search filter is not a screen eitherAdobe’s exclude-generative filter only removes what a contributor declared, and it is applied inconsistently. In this library’s sourcing, three separate searches — for a woman engineer, an analyst at monitors, and a factory supervisor — returned no undeclared-clean results at all: every single result was generative. Check each candidate individually before buying.
Licence before it shipsEvery thumbnail on this page is a watermarked comp. Nothing in shortlist/ may appear in anything that goes out, however finished the layout looks — the card is a decision aid, not a licence.
Model releasesAny image containing recognisable people needs one. Confirm the release covers the intended use, including any paid or out-of-home placement.
ResolutionHover a card for its dimensions. Check the crop you actually intend still leaves enough pixels; don’t judge on the raw number alone.
Exclusivity, where the brand is exposedThe failure mode for a hero image is not a mediocre photo, it is the same photo on a competitor’s site. The premium-collection frames here — Stocksy, Westend61, Blend Images, Marko Geber, Davor — are far less widely used, so put them where the brand is most visible and take the rest where the stakes are lower.

Using an image

RuleRequirement
CroppingCrop freely, but keep the subject. Don’t crop so tight that a photograph of people becomes a photograph of a shoulder. Hover a card for the reviewer’s crop note.
ColorUse them as supplied. No filters, no duotone, no recoloring to force a photo toward the palette.
Type over imageOnly over a quiet, dark area, in the reversed logo’s white. If it needs a heavy scrim to read, pick a different image.
ResolutionDon’t scale a small image up — go back to the source and take a larger render.
RightsOnly licensed images may ship. A card on this page is not a licence, and neither is a watermarked comp.
